---
title: "Can We Even Achieve Autonomous Air Traffic Control?"
description: "Why autonomous air traffic control feels surprisingly close and very far away at the same time."
lang: en
---

We are living through an unusually exciting period of artificial intelligence. Machines can write, see, reason, generate videos, and increasingly act in the physical world. For me, autonomous driving is still one of the most fascinating examples. I simply enjoy watching it work. It also makes me think about air traffic control.

When I was training to become an air traffic controller, and later during my graduate research, I kept coming back to a simple question:

> **If I could learn how to do air traffic control, why couldn't a machine?**

I learned procedures, separation standards, phraseology, traffic patterns, and how to make decisions from a traffic situation. None of this makes air traffic control easy, but it does make autonomous ATC feel possible, at least in principle. Autonomous driving makes me even more optimistic. Then I think about what an air traffic controller actually does, and it suddenly feels very far away again.

## A. What Exactly Is the Machine Supposed to Optimize?

It is tempting to compare autonomous driving with autonomous ATC. Both involve moving agents, uncertainty, prediction, safety, and control. But one difference bothers me more than whether aircraft move in three dimensions. A self-driving car mostly decides what it should do itself:

> **What should my vehicle do next?**

Air traffic control decides what everyone should do:

> **What should all of these aircraft do next?**

That difference is more fundamental than it first appears. Suppose Aircraft A turns left. That turn may determine whether Aircraft B can descend. Keeping B high may then change the sequence of Aircraft C, and that sequence may change what should happen to Aircraft D several minutes later. The actions are not independent. ATC is not the same control problem solved many times in parallel. It is a **joint control problem** in which one aircraft's action changes the future decision space of the others.

Loosely, an individual agent solves

$$
a_i^* = \arg\min_{a_i} J_i,
$$

whereas ATC solves something closer to

$$
\mathbf{A}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{A}} J(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{A}), \qquad \mathbf{A} = (a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_N),
$$

where $\mathbf{A}$ is a coordinated set of actions across the traffic scene.

Autonomous driving is of course not this simple. Other vehicles strongly influence the optimal action, and multi-agent interaction is a major part of autonomous driving research. But the autonomous vehicle normally has no authority over those other vehicles. The controller does. Interestingly, this authority makes one part of ATC easier than driving. A self-driving car must guess what other drivers intend, and those drivers may not cooperate. For IFR traffic in controlled airspace, the intentions of other aircraft are largely what the controller told them to do. Most of the uncertainty about intent disappears, and what remains is uncertainty in execution: wind, aircraft performance, pilot response delay, and occasional misunderstanding. The cost of this authority is responsibility. The system does not only react to the traffic configuration. It **creates the next one**. This immediately raises a surprisingly difficult question:

> **What is the objective function?**

For autonomous driving, we can at least imagine something like

$$
J_{\text{drive}} = J_{\text{collision}} + \lambda_1 J_{\text{progress}} + \lambda_2 J_{\text{comfort}} + \lambda_3 J_{\text{rules}}.
$$

The details are difficult, but the direction is intuitive. What is the equivalent for an air traffic controller? Keep aircraft separated. Sure. But separation alone is not ATC. Get every aircraft to the airport as quickly as possible? That is not ATC either. Controllers implicitly balance separation, sequencing, delay, path efficiency, workload, sector transitions, predictability, and probably many other things that are difficult even to write down. A controller may give one aircraft a longer route so that three other aircraft become easier to sequence. An aircraft may stay at an apparently inefficient altitude because that altitude preserves a useful option several minutes later. A controller may prefer a slightly less efficient solution because the resulting traffic picture is easier to understand and less sensitive to uncertainty. These decisions have value only when viewed as part of the **whole evolving system**. Maybe something like

$$
J_{\text{ATC}} = J_{\text{safety}} + \alpha J_{\text{efficiency}} + \beta J_{\text{workload}} + \gamma J_{\text{coordination}} + \cdots
$$

But what are $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$? Do they stay constant when traffic becomes dense? What exactly is *coordination*, and what is *manageable traffic*? Does safety belong in the objective at all, or should it be a hard constraint? How much inefficiency is reasonable if it makes the traffic situation easier to manage five minutes later?

Even the hard-constraint view is less clean than it looks. Separation minima, such as 3 or 5 nautical miles of radar separation, are not physical laws. They were set to cover the uncertainty of a particular system: surveillance accuracy, communication delay, and human reaction time. An autonomous system with different uncertainty might justify different margins. In practice, it would still be held to standards designed around human operators. So even the most "objective" part of the objective already encodes assumptions about humans.

The objective also depends on its scope. Airspace is divided into sectors, each with its own controller. Aircraft are handed from one sector to the next, coordination follows letters of agreement, and traffic flow management imposes constraints from above. A sector that looks well managed locally can still pass a poorly spaced stream to its neighbor, so good local decisions do not necessarily add up to a good global decision. The partition exists largely because one human cannot hold an entire region's traffic in mind. A machine may not need the same partition, but the surrounding system is built around it. Optimizing one sector, one facility, or the whole network defines three different problems, and it is not obvious which one an autonomous controller should solve.

The terms that are hardest to name may matter most. Consider "keeping options open." One way to read it is that a good controller does not just pick a conflict-free plan. The controller picks a state from which many conflict-free plans remain reachable, so that a wind change or a late pilot response does not force an abrupt maneuver. The value of a state then depends on the set of futures it still allows, not only on the cost of one planned future. That property is hard to score from a single trajectory, and a model that minimizes delay can easily optimize it away.

Similarly, "easy to understand" is not decoration. A human controller must keep an accurate mental model of the traffic, and a configuration that is hard to model is dangerous even if it is currently separated. For a machine, part of this term may disappear. Part of it will not, because pilots and neighboring controllers still need to model the traffic. I return to this point in the next section.

Human controllers answer these questions continuously without writing down an objective function. Perhaps they are not minimizing a clean scalar objective at all. Perhaps they continuously construct traffic states that are **safe now, efficient enough, and still controllable later**. The difficulty is therefore not that ATC has more terms in its objective than autonomous driving. It is that the quality of one action cannot be evaluated without the other aircraft, the future traffic state, and the decisions that will become possible later. That may be one of the first major difficulties of autonomous ATC:

> **Before teaching a machine how to control traffic, we may not even know precisely what "good control" means.**

## B. Should It Be Human-Level or Human-Like?

Suppose we somehow solve that problem and build an AI controller. What should success look like?

Autonomous driving is usually discussed in terms of human-level or superhuman performance. Is the system safer than a human driver? Does it violate fewer traffic rules? Does it react faster to danger? We generally do not require an autonomous vehicle to behave exactly like a human, and we probably do not want it to. This suggests an attractive idea for ATC: forget imitation. If an AI maintains separation better, reduces delay, and handles more traffic than humans, perhaps that is enough.

ATC has an additional complication. The controller does not directly move the aircraft. Another intelligent agent stands between the decision and the physical action: **the pilot**. A self-driving vehicle roughly follows

$$
\text{decision} \rightarrow \text{actuation},
$$

whereas air traffic control looks more like

$$
\text{decision} \rightarrow \text{communication} \rightarrow \text{human interpretation} \rightarrow \text{execution}.
$$

This chain constrains what the AI can do, not only how it explains itself. Controllers issue instructions in standard phraseology: a heading, an altitude, a speed, a direct-to clearance, a hold. The action space is effectively the set of short instructions that a pilot can hear once, read back, and execute without confusion. A trajectory that is optimal in continuous space may require many small, frequent adjustments that no pilot could follow and no radio frequency could carry. Whatever the AI plans must be expressible in the existing language of ATC.

The problem does not end with the pilot. An automated controller will probably not operate in a fully automated sky on day one. Aircraft will leave its sector and enter one controlled by a human. Neighboring sectors will still be run by people, and pilots will need to anticipate what the automated system is likely to do next. The traffic that an AI hands over becomes the initial condition of the next human controller's problem.

Now imagine an AI system with excellent numerical performance whose traffic patterns look completely strange to human controllers. Separation is perfect, average delay is lower, and every instruction is technically valid. But pilots cannot anticipate the instructions, and the receiving controller has no idea why the traffic was arranged that way. That controller then cannot tell which parts of the arrangement are intentional and which parts are a developing problem. Is that human-level autonomous ATC? By some metrics, yes. Operationally, maybe not.

In a mixed system, predictability is a safety property in its own right. Many failures would not come from the AI making a wrong decision. They would come from a human acting reasonably on expectations that the AI does not meet. An instruction that is safe in isolation can be unsafe if the people around it cannot anticipate it.

This makes me think that **human-level** and **human-like** are not quite the right endpoints. The system may not need to reason like a human controller or reproduce human decisions. But it probably has to be **human-compatible**: understandable to pilots, predictable for other controllers, and consistent enough with the larger operational system that humans can safely interact with it.

Human compatibility is also not a fixed target. It matters most during the transition, when humans and automation share the airspace. As more of the system becomes automated, the requirement may relax, and the system may be allowed to depart further from human patterns. The right objective for autonomous ATC may therefore depend on the deployment stage. A policy that is right for a mostly human system may be wrong for a mostly automated one, and the reverse.

This also gives explainability an unusual role in ATC. We may not need to inspect every neuron or attention weight. What matters is that the output of the AI is itself information consumed by other intelligent agents, and those agents need to infer the intent behind it. In ATC, explainability is less about opening the model and more about whether each instruction makes sense to the people who receive it.

> **The mathematically optimal ATC policy may not be the operationally desirable ATC policy.**

## C. We Have the Data. Or Do We?

At this point, machine learning seems to offer one enormous advantage: we have a lot of air traffic data. ADS-B alone gives us vast collections of trajectories, and we can observe aircraft moving through real airspace for hours, days, months, and years. At first this sounds almost ideal. But these data show us what happened. They do not necessarily show us why it happened.

Consider an uneventful pair of trajectories. The aircraft remain safely separated, and nothing interesting happens. From the data alone, we might conclude that there was never a problem. But perhaps three minutes earlier a controller saw a developing conflict and turned one aircraft twenty degrees. The safe trajectories are then not evidence that no conflict existed. They are evidence that somebody prevented it.

Historical air traffic data are therefore not observations of aircraft moving through the sky. They are observations of aircraft moving through a sky that has already been **continuously modified by human decisions**. The intervention is usually invisible, but its consequence remains in the trajectory. This has several consequences that are easy to underestimate.

First, the actions are largely missing. ADS-B records positions and velocities, not clearances. Voice recordings exist, but they are rarely aligned with trajectories at scale, so we usually have to infer actions from changes in motion. A heading change might be a vector, a published procedure, a pilot request, or a deviation around weather.

Second, even when we recover an action, its cause is often outside the data. Controllers respond to weather, flow restrictions, coordination with adjacent sectors, pilot requests, and their own workload. None of these appear in a trajectory. A model trained on trajectories alone will attribute each decision to whatever it can see, so it may learn a correlation where the real cause was invisible.

Third, the data mostly cover situations that good controllers allowed to happen. Controllers keep traffic away from dangerous and hard-to-manage configurations, so those configurations are rare in the record. The situations where control matters most are exactly the ones the data under-represent. A policy learned by imitation may perform well inside this well-managed region of the state space. Once its own small errors push the traffic outside that region, it reaches states that no human controller would have created, and it has almost no data for them.

Fourth, the observed trajectory is only one of many futures that could have happened. If the controller had done nothing, what would have happened? If the controller had turned the other aircraft instead, would that have been better? If the descent had been delayed thirty seconds, would the downstream traffic have become easier or harder? The dataset usually cannot tell us.

This creates an awkward distinction between prediction and control. Trajectory prediction asks

$$
X_{\text{past}} \rightarrow X_{\text{future}},
$$

or simply: *What happened next in situations like this?* Autonomous ATC ultimately needs

$$
X_{\text{past}} \rightarrow A,
$$

which is closer to: *What should I do now?* And eventually it needs something even harder:

$$
(X_{\text{past}}, A) \rightarrow X_{\text{future}},
$$

or: *What would happen if I did something different?*

One detail here is easy to miss. A trajectory prediction model trained on historical data is already, implicitly, a model of the controller. To predict where an aircraft will be in five minutes, the model has to predict the vectors and descents that the controller is likely to issue. Good prediction therefore does not mean that the model lacks control knowledge. It means that the model has absorbed the historical control policy without representing it explicitly. Such a model can tell us what controllers usually do. It cannot tell us what would happen under an action they did not take, because the action was never a separate input.

A controller, however, must reason about actions that were **not taken**. Prediction asks about the observed future. Control asks about alternative futures. That is why even an enormous trajectory dataset may contain much less supervision for autonomous ATC than it appears to.

Simulators seem like the natural answer, because they let us try actions that were never taken. But an ATC simulator must model how pilots respond to instructions, how aircraft deviate from their plans, and how neighboring controllers react. These are the same human behaviors that the data could not reveal. A simulator does not remove the problem. It moves the problem into the simulator's assumptions, and a policy optimized in that simulator may learn to exploit those assumptions rather than control real traffic well.

So perhaps the strange situation is this:

> **We may have an enormous amount of air traffic data, while still having surprisingly little data about air traffic control.**

## So How Far Away Is It?

I think this explains why autonomous ATC feels both close and very far away. We already have powerful AI models, enormous amounts of traffic data, realistic simulators, and decades of operational knowledge. But before a machine can truly control air traffic, we still have to answer some surprisingly basic questions:

1. **Who exactly is the agent?**
2. **What should it optimize?**
3. **How different from humans are we willing to let it behave?**
4. **Can we learn good decisions from data that mostly show us the decisions humans already made?**

These questions are not independent. What counts as a good traffic situation depends on who must understand it. How far we can depart from human behavior depends on whether we can evaluate actions that humans never took. And what we can learn from data depends on what we decided "good" means in the first place. An answer to one question changes the others.

The more I think about autonomous ATC, the more I feel that the main obstacle may not be building a larger neural network. It may be figuring out what problem we actually want that neural network to solve.
